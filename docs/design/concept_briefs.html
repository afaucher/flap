Flap · design sheet · combat · round 2

Concept briefs

Before generating more art, each idea worked through on paper: its premise, the settings it could use, the moment worth showing, how the frame and HUD should be laid out, and what each HUD element tells the player. The prompts at the end of each brief carry that layout. Follows the combat concepts board.

What the first round taught us

  • Say where the camera is, twice. "Chase camera" alone got a bird facing the camera. Ask for "seen from directly behind, we see its back and tail", and place it in the frame ("lower third").
  • Count things in words and numbers. "Five birds" became six. "Exactly five (5) birds, each different" holds better.
  • Keep HUD text to numbers and icons. Generators misspell words. Short numeric labels ("24 m", "3/6") survive; words don't.
  • Ask for the light. The nanite frame was right in spirit and far too dark. Name the time of day and say "bright, high-key daylight" when it matters.
  • Give our scale. Our weapons work at 10–40 m. A HUD reading "310 m" teaches the wrong game.
  • One moment per image. A frame that shows a single readable action (the pass, the snatch, the shot) beats a busy montage.

Round two: what came back

One image per concept, from each brief's first prompt. All four follow the camera and lighting instructions this time. The generator still adds HUD elements we didn't ask for (health bars, a heat gauge) and changes labels.

Where this leaves the concepts

  • Hedgerow Aces has the most character and fits ranged combat without losing the birds. It's the one to push.
  • Nanite crows works in daylight and keeps the real-bird game intact until the moment of attack.
  • Bloodsport is the most spectacular, but its giants would need a different flight model (soaring, few wingbeats).
  • The hunting ground is the closest to the game we have, and the least distinctive as an image.
  • Every image makes the targets large and close. At our real distances a rival is a few pixels wide, which is where trails and brackets earn their keep. The next round should show one fight at true scale.

Direction: Aces in the open forest

The leaning is Hedgerow Aces, set in a sparse forest rather than open farmland. Tall trunks spaced well apart give a lot of vertical room: fights climb and dive through layers instead of staying on one plane, and every trunk and branch is a perch, an ambush point or cover. It's also the arena we already have (understory, canopy and emergent trees, with clearings and lanes), so the art direction and the game converge.

The forest at pilot scale

  • Layers. Ferns and saplings near the ground, an open middle of bare trunks, a high canopy with gaps of sky. Each layer plays differently: cover low, speed in the middle, height above.
  • Perches as outposts. Branch platforms bolted to trunks: a nest-box hangar, ammo crates, a windsock, rope ladders. Landing is how you restock, so outposts are worth fighting over.
  • Landmarks. A lightning-split giant, a fallen log bridge, a woodpecker-holed snag. Pilots navigate by them.
  • Light. Shafts of sun through the canopy, dappled ground, a bright sky above. It keeps the realistic look and stays readable.

Round four: Aces in the open forest

The four forest prompts above, with the round-three fixes. Three of four now get the camera, the single enemy and the projectile direction right; the forest settings all read as open, vertical space to fight in.

What this settles

  • The look. Bright open forest with a chase camera behind a tiny pilot works. Beech and birch read best; the trunks give scale and cover without closing the sky.
  • The HUD. Lock cone, lock diamond with range, lead circle and ammo count are enough. They match the HUD in the combat range.
  • The outpost. A platform with a hangar box, crates and ground crew makes the restock perch a place you fly back to.
  • Still to pin down. The launcher's design (the model keeps inventing guns), and wingtip trails: they should be short, a second long, and start at the wingtips.

  • Flak placement. "Directly in the sparrow's flight path, just ahead of it".

Concept A · physical · realistic

The hunting ground

Real birds, real behaviour. Fights are hunts and thefts: snatch prey, steal it in the air, carry it home. The HUD stays light and close to the bird.

Concept B · ranged · riders

Hedgerow Aces

Thumb-sized pilots fly real birds as war mounts. Each bird wears a leather-and-brass saddle with a launcher bolted in front of the rider over the bird's shoulders: a rocket rack, a repeating dart gun, a little flak mortar. The world is ordinary countryside at our scale, which for them is enormous: hedgerows are fortress walls, a birdhouse is an airfield, a washing line is a tripwire. It looks like a First World War air war: rivets, goggles, canvas, smoke.

Why it fits

  • Our birds stay real birds; the war gear sits on top, so the flight model and plumage still matter.
  • Ranged combat gets a fiction that's warm, not grim, and a reason for short ranges: tiny weapons.
  • Ammo and repairs at the birdhouse airfield are the nest loop.
  • A rider knocked off can drift down on a dandelion-seed parachute. That's a second way to win a fight: unseat the pilot, not the bird.

HUD, built into the saddle

  • The launcher's ring-and-bead gunsight is the reticle. A faint second bead is the lead marker.
  • Brass dials on the saddle front: airspeed and altitude. A small ammo drum counter: "4".
  • A heartbeat gauge for the bird's stamina.
  • A paper wing chart clipped to the saddle, with two lost feathers inked out.
  • A squadron pennant score along the top edge, and small brass chevrons pointing to birds off-screen.

Concept C · ranged · nanite

Nanite crows, in daylight

Crows picked up a nanotech swarm from a crashed delivery drone. They look like ordinary crows until they act: then seams of light run along their feather shafts, and they throw shards, swarm clouds and arcs at each other over territory. The human world goes on around them, which is the joke and the charm. Setting it in daylight keeps it readable and less like every other neon game; the glow is reserved for the moment of attack.

Concept D · physical · riders

Bloodsport

Gene-edited giants, eight to ten metres across, raced and jousted by human riders for prizes and crowds. Birds that size glide and soar more than they flap, so the course is built around updrafts. The fight is a series of passes: line up, brace the lance, strike or swerve, climb back on a thermal.

The moment

From directly behind our rider, mid-pass: the rival rider is coming head-on through a ring gate with the lance lowered, about 30 m out.

HUD

  • A joust timing ring around the rival that shrinks to the moment to brace.
  • Seat meters for both riders: how close each is to being unhorsed.
  • Pass counter "2/3" and a crowd meter along the top.
  • An updraft arrow on the course ahead.
  • Speed and glide at the bottom left; no flapping energy, since giants soar.

Other directions worth a sketch

  • Mouse cavalry. Hedgerow Aces with mice instead of tiny people: softer, storybook, the same saddle-and-launcher idea.
  • Ornithopter drones. No living birds: military bird-shaped drones flown remotely in a real landscape. Clean daylight, wire-frame HUD. It loses the plumage work.
  • Falconry duels. Human falconers on the ground direct their birds against each other: a two-layer game of commands and flying.
