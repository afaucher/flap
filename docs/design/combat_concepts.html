Combat concepts

Two ways birds could fight in Flap, what each would ask of the game we already have, how to make small fast targets readable, and three art directions with mock frames of what a fight might look like in each.

Two ways to fight

Physical: talons and bodiesRanged: augmented birds

Physical: borrow what birds really do

Real raptors and corvids already have a combat vocabulary. Each behaviour becomes one mechanic, and most reuse code that exists.

BehaviourIn natureMechanicBuilt from
Ground snatchA hawk drops on a vole; the vole bolts when the shadow crosses it.Prey on the ground or a branch. You need to arrive inside the talon reach while moving under a snatch speed. Prey flee from a shadow or a visible approach, so come in low, from the sun, or from behind cover.The catch envelope and perch assist, with a faster speed limit than landing.
Stoop strikeA peregrine hits prey with its feet at the bottom of a dive.A high-energy pass. A hit knocks the target into a short stun and tumble (loss of control, height lost). Miss and you've spent your height.The stall and stun states, the dive band.
Talon grappleEagles lock feet and cartwheel, letting go before the ground.Both birds tumble together. A short contest of flaps and pulls decides who breaks free on top. Hold on too long and both hit the ground.A new two-bird state; the energy model pays for it.
Stealing foodEagles chase ospreys until they drop the fish; frigatebirds harass boobies.A bird carrying prey is heavier, so its stall speed and flapping cost rise. Chase it, strike it, and it drops the prey to catch mid-air or on the ground. Carry food home to the nest to score.Mass in the flight model already drives stall speed and power.
MobbingCrows dive at a hawk until it leaves.Small birds harass a raptor. Each close pass drains its stamina and spoils its strikes. It's a way for a sparrow to fight a hawk.The energy model's stamina.

To keep contact hard and rare without making it random, a hit uses the same rule as a perch catch. The target must be inside the talon reach, measured relative to your flight line, while the closing speed is inside a window. The window is wide for a stoop strike, narrow for a snatch and narrowest for a grapple. Prey see you coming, and AI targets break when you close.

Ranged: short weapons that make you chase

Keep every weapon short-ranged and slow enough to dodge, so the fight stays a pursuit: you follow, cut inside the turn, and fire when the shot is set up.

The nest

Ammo is a handful of rounds. You restock at your home nest, which is also where repairs happen and food is scored. That gives each fight a rhythm: sortie, engage, break off, return. Raiding someone else's nest becomes a goal in itself.

Weight

Weapons are payload. A full load adds 10–15% to the bird's mass, which raises stall speed and the cost of flapping. A fresh bird is sluggish; a bird with an empty rack flies like itself again.

Damage you can see

A hit knocks feathers out. The rig already lays out every feather, so a hit hides the struck ones. The flight model loses that wing area, and the bird rolls toward its damaged side. You can read the damage from behind and feel it in the stick. Feathers grow back at the nest.

Energy fighting

Height is stored speed. A falcon wins by diving through, firing, and climbing away. A sparrow wins by turning inside the dart. The species differences we already simulate become fighting styles.

Seeing the fight

Targets are small and fast. At 20 m a crow is a few pixels wide, and you can't judge its turn from its body alone. These aids help in both modes.

Wingtip trails: yes

  • Two ribbons, one from each wingtip, showing the last second of flight. A turn shows as a curve before the bird's attitude reads.
  • They fade with age and narrow toward the tail. They thicken in a hard pull, since real wingtip vortices get stronger with lift.
  • Invisible within 4 m and fully shown by 15 m, so they help at range without cluttering a close fight.
  • On for other birds by default, coloured by side (rival, ally, prey); optional for your own bird.
  • Each theme draws them its own way: pale vapour in the field, glowing nanite streams, cloth streamers on bloodsport mounts.

Focus mode

  • Hold the right trigger or right mouse button: the field of view narrows from about 70° to 40°, and the camera swings to keep you and the nearest target in frame.
  • Steering eases a little while held. A bracket marks the target with its range and closing speed.
  • In ranged mode, a lead marker shows where to aim for each weapon. In physical mode, it shows the talon reach and the snatch window instead.
  • Off-screen arrows at the screen edge for anything tracked, and a rising whoosh as a threat closes from behind.

Recommendation

Start with physical combat in the realistic look. It fits the game we have, and everything it needs also serves a ranged mode later: tracking a target, judging closing speed, readable trails, a focus camera, hits that change how a bird flies. Prototype ranged weapons in the bird lab once those exist, before deciding on the pivot.

  1. Wingtip trails and focus mode

    Cheap, and they help with the flying we already have: following AI birds and lining up landings.

  2. Ground prey and the snatch window

    Voles in the clearing that bolt from shadows; the catch envelope with a faster speed limit. The first moment of combat.

  3. Carrying and stealing

    Prey adds mass; a strike makes it drop. Food carried home to the nest scores. That makes a full loop.

  4. Strikes, stun and feather damage

    Stoop strikes and the visible feather-loss damage model, shared by both directions.

  5. A ranged test in the lab

    Micro-rockets against an AI crow, with ammo and the nest. Then decide between nanobot crows and the bloodsport.

Open questions

  • Against AI only, or other players? That decides how much of this has to work over a network.
  • Is the goal territory (owning perches and nests), score (food home), or survival?
  • For bloodsport: can the rider act on their own (lance, net) or only through the bird?

Physics notes for the giants

  • Weight grows with the cube of size and wing area with the square, so a ten-metre bird has a high stall speed and can barely flap. The largest flying bird known, Argentavis (about 6–7 m), is thought to have mostly soared.
  • So the bloodsport plays as gliding and soaring: canyon updrafts, thermals, long jousting passes, few wingbeats.
