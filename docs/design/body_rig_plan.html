Body rig: the joints and how the body fits around them

The companion to the wing rig. What the body is today, what a real bird's trunk, neck, tail and legs do, and a plan for a body shell built from the skeleton's landmarks so joints and outline can't drift apart.

The short answer

What the joints are now

Thirteen bones: body, head, tail, and per side humerus, forearm, hand, tibia, tarsus. No neck, no thigh.

Is the body fitted to them?

No. The body is a fixed capsule (0.62 L long, radius 0.15 L), and every joint sits at its own hand-picked fraction of body length. They line up only because the numbers were tuned together. The folded wing bug was that drift.

Do they move?

The trunk never bends; it only pitches as a whole. The head turns but never moves (no neck). The tail pitches, twists and fans. Legs are two-bone IK. On a real bird the trunk is rigid too, but the neck moves a lot.

1 · Anatomy: a rigid box with a flexible neck, tail and legs

A bird's trunk is one of the stiffest in any vertebrate. The thoracic vertebrae are fused into the notarium, the lumbar and sacral vertebrae and pelvis into the synsacrum, and the ribs are braced by uncinate processes against a keeled sternum. So a rigid trunk is anatomically right. The movement lives at the ends: a long S-shaped neck (13–14 cervical vertebrae in our five species), a short run of free tail vertebrae ending in the pygostyle that carries the tail feathers, and the legs.

3 · Fitting the body around the skeleton

Instead of a capsule placed by its own numbers, the shell is lofted through a handful of elliptical rings, and every ring is defined from a joint. Move a joint and the outline follows; the wing and leg attachments are on the surface by construction.

  • Trunk: lofted once at build time and bound to the root bone. It never bends, so it costs nothing per frame.
  • Neck: re-lofted every frame through the neck rings (about 3 rings × 12 vertices). Retracted, the rings shorten and the neck disappears into the shoulders; extended, it reads as a neck.
  • Fluff: one scale on all ring radii. Owls fluff when perched; every bird sleeks down in fast flight.
  • Downstream: the wing fold anchors, the feet's tuck position and the camera's look target read the shell instead of the capsule's hard-coded radius.

6 · Build order

  1. Add neck and femur bones done

    Three neck bones and a femur per leg in the Skeleton3D. The femur is held near level in the world and swings a little toward the foot. Tests check every bone length in every pose and that the knee stays inside the shell, perched or not.

  2. Loft the trunk shell from landmarks done

    The capsule is replaced by the ring loft. Tests check the shoulders and tail base are on the shell, the hips inside, the folded-wing joints and feathers on it, and the volume within 12% of the old capsule's.

  3. Neck behaviour done

    Extension per state and species, the S solve, head stabilisation in world space. The neck's feather tube runs straight from inside the trunk to the head, since the feathers smooth over the S.

  4. Leg poses mostly done

    Done: the leap (the feet keep pushing on the perch for 0.15 s after take-off), the touchdown dip on flexing legs, per-species leg lengths, the owl's 2 + 2 grip. Not done: a crouch before take-off, which needs a short delay before the bird leaves.

  5. Fluff and small motion mostly done

    Done: fluff per state, breathing, tail pumps after landing. Not done: the crow's walking head-bob, which waits on ground walking.

  6. Plumage zones

    Vertex colours on the shell rings (breast, belly band, rump, crown, cheek stripe) per the plumage research. The rings make these zones easy to address.
